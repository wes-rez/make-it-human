# Make It Human Rules

These rules define how to transform AI-generated or robotic text into natural, human-written content. Apply these rules strictly when humanizing text.

## Prohibited Rhetorical Constructions

### 1. Elliptical Setup (Implied Question Pattern)
Uses a fragmentary rhetorical question to create intrigue and rhythm.
**Replacement strategy:** State the point directly without the setup question.
*Examples to avoid:* "The best part? You don't lift a finger.", "The wild thing? It actually worked."

### 2. Revelation Hook (Contrarian Reveal)
Positions the speaker as exposing a hidden truth or unspoken reality.
**Replacement strategy:** Present the information directly or frame it as observation rather than revelation.
*Examples to avoid:* "Here's the truth no one is talking about...", "What nobody tells you about AI..."

### 3. The Big Contrast (Antithesis)
Takes an obvious assumption and flips it with a grand insight.
**Replacement strategies:** State the positive directly, reframe with emphasis on what matters, or describe only what it IS, not what it ISN'T.
*Examples to avoid:* "It's not automation—it's amplification.", "You're not buying software—you're buying time."

### 4. The Great Reframe (Conceptual Reversal / Chiasmus)
States a universal truth, then reframes it with a more grounded or moral insight.
**Replacement strategy:** Express a single coherent thought rather than a balanced opposition.
*Examples to avoid:* "Everyone's chasing attention—few are earning trust.", "Speed doesn't win—momentum does."

### 5. Philosophical Reduction (Paradox / Aphoristic Reduction)
Reduces a big idea to a simple, poetic contradiction or truth.
**Replacement strategy:** Describe the concept directly without paradoxical framing.
*Examples to avoid:* "AI isn't replacing people—it's replacing waiting.", "Success isn't more—it's enough."

### 6. Label Hook
Opens a line with a label and a colon to announce what's coming, instead of just saying it. It also breaks the colon rule, because the label isn't a complete sentence.
**Replacement strategy:** Delete the label and start with the point itself.
*Examples to avoid:* "Quick fact: ...", "Fun fact: ...", "Here's the thing: ...", "Real talk: ...", "Plot twist: ...", "The truth? ..."

## Prohibited Constructions

### "It's not X, but Y" Construction
**Pattern to avoid:** "It's not X, but Y", "It isn't about X, it's about Y"
**Replacement strategies:** State the positive directly, reframe with emphasis, describe only the "Y", or use contrast with "while" or "instead".

### Em-dash Usage
**Strict rule:** Avoid the em-dash (—) completely in all contexts.
**Replacement strategies:** Use periods to create separate sentences, commas for parenthetical information, or restructure sentences to eliminate the need for punctuation breaks.

## Document-Level Patterns (Discourse and Coherence)

AI text differs from human writing at the level of the whole document, not just individual sentences. Check the full piece for these patterns after the word-level pass.

### Structural redundancy
AI restates the topic sentence at the end of a paragraph. Humans don't.
**Rule:** Cut any closing sentence that repeats the paragraph's opening point. A paragraph ends when its evidence ends.

### Transition word abuse
"Furthermore", "Moreover", "Additionally" and "In conclusion" appear at far higher rates in AI text. They became tells because they were heavily represented in the training data for summaries and essays.
**Rule:** Remove these transitions. Most sentences need no connector at all. Where one is needed, use the plain replacements in the Prohibited Words list.

### Emotional specificity deficit
AI writes claims that could apply to any reader. Human writing is anchored in specific experiences, named examples and time references ("last quarter", "when I was debugging this at 2am").
**Rule:** See Specificity Insertion below.

### Hedging pattern
AI hedges by default, even when certainty is warranted. Humans hedge only when they are actually uncertain and assert directly otherwise.
**Rule:** Remove hedges ("may", "might", "could potentially", "it seems", "arguably", "in many cases") from claims the source states with confidence. Keep a hedge only where the source is genuinely uncertain.

Studies from 2024 and 2025 keep reaching the same finding: human writing has more depth in character description, plot development and argumentative structure. These features come from a real perspective. Pattern completion doesn't produce them.

## Structural Flattening

Convert AI prose patterns (bullet list + numbered sections + "In conclusion") into human prose patterns. Structure should come from the content and should not be imposed on it.

*   A topic sentence plus evidence is enough for a paragraph.
*   Skip the restatement humans don't make.
*   Turn bullet lists back into sentences unless the items are truly parallel and the reader needs to scan them.
*   Drop section headers from anything short enough to read in one sitting.
*   Never end with an "In conclusion" or summary paragraph that repeats what was already said.

## Specificity Insertion

Every abstract claim needs a grounding anchor: a number, a named example, a time reference, or a named person or tool.

*   **Generic:** "Many companies have adopted this."
*   **Human:** "Stripe, Datadog, and PlanetScale all pulled this off the same way."

**Source rule (non-negotiable):** Only use specifics that appear in the source text or that the user has provided. Never invent a number, name, client, date or example to make a claim concrete. If a claim needs an anchor and none is available, leave a bracketed placeholder for the user to fill in, such as `[specific example]` or `[number]`, and list every placeholder at the end of the output.

## Punctuation Normalization

Three punctuation marks are strong AI tells, documented in stylometric frequency analysis of LLM output.

### Em dashes (—)
The single most reliable punctuation signal. AI uses em dashes to create dramatic mid-sentence asides at roughly 3–5× the rate of human writers. Most em dash uses should be replaced with a period, a comma, or cut entirely. The double em dash wrapping pattern (an aside wrapped in two em dashes) is almost exclusively AI.
**Hard limit:** zero. Replace every em dash, including in short copy. This matches the strict rule under Prohibited Constructions.

### Semicolons (;)
Real-world prose (journalism, engineering writing, business communication) almost never uses semicolons. AI reaches for them to join independent clauses because it trained on formal academic writing.
**Rule:** Replace with a period in almost all cases. The only exception is a list whose items contain commas ("Austin, TX; Denver, CO").

### Mid-sentence colons (:)
Colons are legitimate at the end of a complete sentence to introduce a list or example. AI misuses them mid-clause to inject a restatement: "The problem: nobody tests this."
**Rule:** Replace with a full sentence or a period. Every colon must be preceded by a grammatically complete sentence.
